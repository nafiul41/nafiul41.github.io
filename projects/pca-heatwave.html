Overview

This project has two parts. The first part covers the theory of Principal Component Analysis (PCA): why the principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, how to choose how many to keep, and where PCA works well or badly. The second part applies PCA to a real climate problem: predicting the number of annual Heat Wave Days (HWDs) in each of Bangladesh's eight administrative divisions from ERA5 reanalysis data for 1940–2025.

The prediction framework combines mRMR feature selection, PCA, six regression models and SHAP explanations. It makes predictions from compressed features and also shows which atmospheric variables drive heat waves in each division.

The theory of PCA

PCA turns many correlated variables into a smaller set of new, uncorrelated variables called principal components. Each component is a weighted combination of the original variables, and the components are ordered by how much of the data's variance they capture. The method goes back to Karl Pearson (1901), and Harold Hotelling named it in 1933.

Why the answer is an eigenvector

Project each mean-centred observation xi onto a unit direction w, giving the score zi = w⊤xi. The variance of these scores is

Var(z) = w⊤Σw,  subject to w⊤w = 1

where Σ is the covariance matrix. Maximising this with a Lagrange multiplier gives

Σw = λw

so the best direction is an eigenvector of Σ, and the variance along it equals its eigenvalue λ. Writing any unit vector in the eigenvector basis shows that the variance can never exceed the largest eigenvalue λ1. That maximum is reached only at its eigenvector v1, the first principal component. Each following component captures the most remaining variance while staying orthogonal to the earlier ones.

The steps

  1. Standardise each feature: z = (x − μ) / σ.
  2. Compute the covariance matrix Σ = Z⊤Z / (n − 1).
  3. Find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors, ΣV = VΛ.
  4. Sort them so that λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ … ≥ λp.
  5. Keep the top k eigenvectors, Vk.
  6. Project the data: Y = Z Vk, reducing p features to k.

How many components to keep

  • Explained variance ratio: the share of total variance each component captures, λi / Σλj.
  • Cumulative explained variance: keep enough components to reach a target, such as 95%. This project uses that rule.
  • Scree plot: stop at the "elbow", where adding components stops helping much.
  • Kaiser criterion: keep components with eigenvalue greater than 1.

Strengths and weaknesses

Strengths Weaknesses
Reduces the number of dimensions while keeping most of the variance Eigen- or SVD decomposition is costly for very large datasets
Removes multicollinearity: the components are uncorrelated Sensitive to outliers, because it relies on the covariance matrix
Lets high-dimensional data be plotted in 2D or 3D Unsupervised: it ignores the target variable
Fast, with nothing to tune Linear only; non-linear structure needs Kernel PCA or t-SNE
Filters noise by dropping low-variance components Components mix the original variables and are hard to interpret

The report also covers a geometric example (bean shape features rotated onto principal axes) and PCA's uses in computer vision, finance, genomics, and weather and climate science.

A worked example

Before the full climate data, the report runs PCA by hand on six days of synthetic weather data with three features: temperature, relative humidity and wind speed.

Component Eigenvalue Explained variance Cumulative
PC1 61.433 92.99% 92.99%
PC2 3.759 5.69% 98.68%
PC3 0.875 1.33% 100%

The first two components keep 98.7% of the variance, so the three features can be replaced by two. PC1 is mostly humidity (loading 0.913), with wind speed on the same side and temperature opposite: a combined moisture-and-wind axis. PC2 is mostly wind speed (0.931). The climate model applies the same idea to much larger sets of pressure-level variables.

Data

The application uses ERA5, ECMWF's global reanalysis, downloaded through the Copernicus Climate Data Store at 0.25° × 0.25°. ERA5 was chosen over station records because it covers every part of the country with no gaps, back to 1940. Of 594 grid points in the 88°–92.5°E, 20.5°–26.5°N box, 197 fall on land in Bangladesh, and each was assigned to a division.

Predictors

Seven variables at four pressure levels (1000, 850, 500 and 300 hPa), averaged over March–May for each division and year, give 28 predictors.

Category Variable Why it matters for heat waves
Temperature Air temperature (t) Directly sets heat-wave occurrence and intensity
Moisture Relative humidity (r), specific humidity (q) Controls evaporative cooling and heat stress
Circulation Zonal and meridional wind (u, v) Transport of hot or dry air, and weak ventilation
Vertical motion Vertical velocity (w) Sinking air suppresses cloud and lets the sun heat the surface
Pressure pattern Geopotential (z) Upper-air ridges bring stable, persistent hot weather

Target: Heat Wave Days

The label follows the Bangladesh Meteorological Department (BMD) definition, adapted to a grid. A day is hot in a division when at least 50% of its grid cells have a daily maximum temperature above 36 °C. Three or more hot days in a row make a heat wave. The heat-wave days in March–May are summed to give each division's annual HWD count.

Method

  1. Preprocessing. ERA5 grid points were clipped to Bangladesh, assigned to divisions, and averaged into one value per variable, pressure level, division and year.
  2. Split by time. 1940–2008 for training and 2009–2025 for testing, so the models are tested on years they have not seen.
  3. Feature selection (mRMR). Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance ranked the 28 features by how much they tell about HWDs without repeating each other. The top 10, 15 and 20 formed three subsets.
  4. Feature extraction (PCA). PCA was applied to each subset separately, keeping enough components for 95% of the variance. This gave three feature sets: PCA (mRMR-10), PCA (mRMR-15) and PCA (mRMR-20).
  5. Models. Six regression models were trained on each feature set: Linear, Ridge, Lasso and Elastic Net regression, Gaussian Process Regression, and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (one hidden layer of 8 ReLU neurons, Adam, L2 = 0.001, early stopping).
  6. Evaluation. Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and the correlation coefficient (CC) between observed and predicted HWDs.
  7. Interpretation (SHAP). SHAP values were computed for the chosen model, using the original Top-20 variables so that each contribution has a physical meaning.

Results

Where heat waves happen

Heat waves follow a clear west-to-east gradient. Rajshahi and Khulna, in the drier west, have the most heat-wave days. Sylhet, Chittagong and Barishal, near the Bay of Bengal and the hills, have the fewest.

How it changed over time

  • 1957 was the worst year on record in seven of the eight divisions: a single, country-wide heat wave.
  • No division shows a significant increase over 1940–2025. Khulna and Mymensingh show significant decreases; elsewhere year-to-year variation dominates.
  • Chittagong has few heat-wave days historically but has risen since 2020, peaking in 2023.

Which model worked best

  • The four linear models (Linear, Ridge, Lasso, Elastic Net) gave almost identical errors. This suggests PCA had already removed the multicollinearity that regularisation would otherwise fix.
  • Ridge Regression had slightly lower errors and was the most stable across divisions, so it was chosen for interpretation.
  • More selected features helped. Ridge's average MAE fell from 4.55 days (Top-10) to 4.23 (Top-15) and 4.08 (Top-20).
  • GPR and the MLP were inconsistent. They won in a few divisions (the MLP in Mymensingh and Sylhet) but did worse elsewhere. With only one sample per year, there was too little data for them to train reliably.

What drives heat waves (SHAP)

SHAP assigns each variable a share of every prediction, based on Shapley values from game theory. It shows which conditions push a year's predicted heat-wave count up or down.

Mymensinghshum_P1000, temp_P850, uwnd_P1000High
Rajshahizpt_P850, temp_P300, temp_P850High
Rangpurshum_P1000, temp_P850, temp_P300High

  • Dry air near the ground is the main driver in six divisions. In Barishal, Chittagong, Dhaka, Khulna, Mymensingh and Rangpur, the top variable is specific humidity at 1000 hPa. Lower humidity means more heat-wave days, because dry air reduces evaporative cooling and heats the surface more.
  • Rajshahi is driven by large-scale circulation. Geopotential height at 850 hPa and upper-air temperature matter most, pointing to high-pressure ridges and sinking air.
  • Sylhet is driven by vertical motion. Vertical velocity at 850 hPa is the top variable, so whether convection is suppressed matters more than humidity there.
  • Khulna's predictions are the most sensitive to atmospheric conditions, with the widest SHAP range.

Limitations & future work

Limitations

  • Small sample. One value per year gives few training samples, which limits data-hungry models such as the MLP and GPR.
  • One season. Only the pre-monsoon (March–May) is studied.
  • Reanalysis, not stations. ERA5 is complete and consistent but is not a direct observation.
  • SHAP on original variables. SHAP was applied to the mRMR-selected variables rather than the PCA features, to keep the explanations physically meaningful.

Future work

  • Extend the analysis to all seasons.
  • Add predictors such as soil moisture, land use and sea-surface temperature.
  • Use data augmentation or transfer learning to stabilise non-linear models.
  • Apply the framework to high-resolution climate projections to assess future heat-wave risk.
